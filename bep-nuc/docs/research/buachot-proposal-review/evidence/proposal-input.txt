Executive summary
BữaChốt is a pickup marketplace that turns small food shops' end-of-day surplus into discounted meals for students living around one campus, while guaranteeing each shop an agreed minimum net payout.
Every evening a student receives three one-use vouchers (7%, 10%, 20%), uses one per order, and sees the final price before confirming. If the student is willing to walk to the shop, a capped pickup bonus raises the voucher (for example 7% to 15% for a 1 km route). A backend price floor ensures that no voucher or bonus can ever push the shop's payout below what it agreed to, and the student never sees that floor.
Our starting hypothesis is that students will collect a meal on foot when the saving is visible in dong, and that shops will list surplus when the payout is certain. Both hypotheses are tested before any app is built: a survey of about 70 students and interviews with 10 to 20 shops near one campus cluster, followed by a manual pilot with 3 to 5 shops. Machine learning is a later phase, used only to tune the bonus once randomized pilot data exists.
All discount percentages, the 5% base discount and the 8-point-per-km bonus are parameters we will tune with field data, not claims of proven effectiveness.
Problem analysis
Two groups near a campus have complementary problems: small food shops end the day with food they cannot sell at full price, and students on tight budgets want a cheap evening meal but face delivery fees and uncertain promotions.
Shops. A shop that prepares food daily almost always has leftovers at closing time. Current options are to throw them away, give them away, discount informally, or list them on a delivery app that takes a commission and requires a rider. None gives the shop a predictable payout for surplus.
Students. Students decide price-first and live within walking distance of many shops. Delivery apps already compete with frequent vouchers, so a surplus offer must beat them on visible savings, not only on sustainability.
Existing alternatives. Too Good To Go already runs a self-collection model with a loyalty programme, so pickup or vouchers alone are not a new idea. BữaChốt's angle is narrower: a local, campus-cluster marketplace where the discount a student can reach and the payout a shop is guaranteed are set by the same pricing rules.

Value proposition
BữaChốt gives students a visible saving in dong, gives shops a guaranteed payout for food they would otherwise lose, and gives the platform a margin on every completed pickup.

What is different. Our proposal combines four things in one campus cluster: a daily set of three vouchers, a capped pickup bonus tied to route distance, a hidden per-shop price floor that protects the shop's payout, and a controlled pilot that measures whether the bonus actually raises completed pickups. The combination is the contribution; each piece alone exists elsewhere.
Stakeholder map
Students and partner shops decide whether the marketplace works, so we involve them at every pilot step; regulators and campus staff can stop it, so we check with them before launch. Placement is our current judgement and will be revised after the interviews.
MVP concept
The MVP is a mobile web app (PWA) plus a manual operations routine for 3 to 5 shops near one campus; it proves that students collect and shops list, before any machine learning is built.
What a student does
1. Opens the app after 18:00 and sees the shops' surplus items, each with its original price, the price after the 5% base discount, and the vouchers that still leave the shop's payout intact.
2. Picks one item and one voucher; if the route to the shop is within the allowed range, the app shows the pickup bonus and the extra dong saved.
3. Pays, then receives a one-time QR code and a pickup window.
4. Shows the QR at the counter; the shop scans it to confirm the pickup and the quantity.
What the backend does
• Holds the shop's net-payout floor privately; the student-facing API returns only prices, eligible vouchers, bonus and final amount.
• Caps every voucher so the shop's payout and the platform's minimum margin are never breached, and refuses to list items that cannot be priced inside the cap.
• Locks the quoted price for a short period, reserves stock with a timeout, and restores the voucher if the shop cancels or runs out.
• Records each order's voucher, bonus, distance band, pickup result and refund so that the later experiment can be analysed.
Left out of the MVP on purpose: delivery, in-app ML, continuous GPS tracking and any CO2 claim. The route distance is computed once at quote time through a maps service, not tracked during the trip.
Pickup bonus rule (a tunable hypothesis). The voucher is raised by 8 percentage points per km of route distance up to a cap, then clipped by the shop's price floor and a platform policy cap, so the discount the student sees is always the one actually applied.
Revenue model and unit economics
The platform earns the difference between what the student pays and what the shop is paid, minus per-order costs; the shop's payout is a fixed share of the original price agreed in advance.
Notation: P is the original price, b = 5% the base discount, v the voucher, F the shop's agreed net payout, C the variable cost per order and m the minimum contribution we require per order. The student pays:
T = P(1-b)(1-v)
The largest voucher that still protects the shop and the platform is:
v_{max} = 1 - \frac{F + C + m}{P(1-b)}
Worked example (illustrative assumptions, not measured results): P = 100,000 VND, F = 65,000 VND (65% of P), C = 3,000 VND, m = 2,000 VND. Then v_max = 26.3%. The value of F is the number our shop interviews must replace with real data.

Contract choice. In the model above the shop receives a fixed payout per item. A second option is for the shop to receive the sale price minus a commission; with a 10% commission and the same 65,000 floor the safe voucher cap falls to about 24.0%. We will choose one settlement model with each shop before the pilot and show it in writing.
Revenue streams after the pilot. Per-order margin first. Later options (only if the pilot supports them): a small listing fee for shops that want more exposure, and campus-partner sponsorship for food-waste or student-budget campaigns.
Go-to-market and validation plan
We launch in one campus cluster, prove that students collect and shops list in a manual pilot, and only then build the app and the learning layer; each stage has a gate that must be passed to continue.
1. Research (now to 27 October 2026). Student survey (about 70) and shop interviews (10 to 20) near the chosen campus. Gate: at least some shops accept a net payout level that leaves a positive margin at a discount students say they want. If not, we change the payout or discount before building anything.
2. Manual pilot (3 to 5 shops). Shops post surplus in a shared sheet or chat group, we price with the formulas above, take payment by simple transfer and confirm pickup with codes. Gate: repeat weekly surplus, pickup rate and no-show rate acceptable to shops.
3. App MVP. Build the PWA, voucher set, price floor, stock reservation and QR pickup described in the MVP section. Gate: orders and settlements run without manual correction.
4. Randomized bonus test. Keep the three vouchers for everyone; randomly assign the extra distance bonus (with a control group) and record every eligible exposure, purchase, no-show and refund. Gate: the bonus raises completed pickups enough to cover its cost.
5. Learning layer. Only after step 4: start with simple pickup-rate tables and logistic regression, then uplift models or contextual bandits to choose the bonus level within the price floor and budget.
Channels. Class and dormitory groups, student clubs, posters at partner shops, and a link or support channel on Zalo where appropriate. First shops are recruited through direct visits, with the net-payout rule explained on one page.
What we measure. Orders per shop per evening, share of surplus rescued, voucher mix (do students move to 7% and 10% after using 20%?), pickup, no-show and refund rates, shop payout and platform contribution per order, and weekly repeat users.

Impact and purpose
BữaChốt aims at three measurable results: money saved by students, income recovered by shops, and meals rescued from the bin.
• Students: a saving of roughly 12% to 24% of the original price per order under the example rules (up to about 26% with the bonus), shown in dong before they decide.
• Shops: a predictable payout for food that would otherwise be lost, with no rider or commission surprise.
• Food waste: the pilot measures portions rescued per shop per evening and whether they replace waste or simply replace full-price sales.
What we do not claim. We do not claim that pickup reduces emissions: if students make a special trip, the net environmental effect can be neutral or negative, so we will measure trip type rather than assume a CO2 saving. We also do not claim that the discount levels or the 8-point bonus are optimal; they are starting values to be tuned with pilot data.
Success criteria for the pilot. Repeat weekly surplus from at least 3 shops, a pickup rate shops accept, a positive contribution per completed order, and evidence from the randomized test that the bonus raises completed pickups.
